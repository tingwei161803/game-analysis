Overview / Shanshenbu Brawl developers

Shanshenbu Brawl

A browser fighter built from the move names of a viral flower-drum-lantern dance lesson: three starter moves, thirty-odd more to slot in, a roguelite climb, gym ladders and Elo ranked play — all inside one 21,725-line HTML file, with rollback netcode and CPU opponents that are self-play-trained neural networks.

Dimension by dimension

The seven dimensions

memoryTechnical Depth 8.5/10

Three techniques that rarely appear in a hobby browser game, all hand-written in one file. Rollback netcode: both peers run the same 60 Hz simulation and exchange only a key bitmask; during a step Math.random is swapped for a seeded mulberry32 (rbStep, index.html:14195), floats are quantized every frame (rbQ, :14187), state is FNV-hashed twice a second (rbHash, :14143) and a mismatch makes the host resend everything; the correction is smoothed only at draw time, never in the simulated values (:14097-14235). Transport falls back in three tiers — two WebRTC DataChannels, one reliable and one with maxRetransmits: 0 (:14530), then Cloudflare TURN, then a WebSocket relay. Neural-network opponents: a plain-JS MLP forward pass with tanh hidden layers and softmax sampling over 18 actions (nnAct, :7982), fed an observation of the board as it was 0.2 s earlier so the model has a human reaction delay (:7906); the two model files inspected are 58-128-128-18 and 359-256-256-18. Procedural animation: every fighter is a skeleton computed from 16 joint angles (skeleton, :3405) with Catmull-Rom interpolation between poses (splinePose, :3293), and the keyframes were extracted from the source video frame by frame.

stadia_controllerGame Design 8/10

Moves are data, not code: each is {cd, dur, keys, ev}, where ev is a timeline of hits, grabs, projectiles, guard windows (invulnerable, block, reflect, counter, parry), buffs, jumps and dives (MOVES, :3900). That makes a thirty-move pool cheap to extend and cheap to retire — thirteen moves are switched off but kept in the table (MOVE_OFF, :3999). Around the fighter sit a roguelite climb with per-stage scaling and a seven-node legacy tree, gym ladders, and Elo ranked play with K=40 for the first ten games and 24 after (rkDelta, :19954). Unusually, the balance comments quote runs: a change to minion caps is justified by ten runs each that died on average at stage 25.0 before and 26.0 after (:15430), and the finding that 'random mashing and spacing out barely weaken a bot — what works is limiting how often it attacks' (:19347) is the kind of result only measurement produces. The same comments are also candid that some numbers were simply chosen.

securityBackend & Security 6/10

A real backend — Workers, a Durable Object, D1, TURN, Google sign-in — that can only be judged from the outside, since worker.js never reaches the browser. What is visible is mixed. On the good side, the author ran a security review on their own code and left the findings in the comments: a #mv= migration import that could overwrite any ssb_* key and, combined with innerHTML, lift an admin key, now restricted to an allow-list plus a referrer check (:32-44); a stored-XSS path through untyped profile fields, closed by profClean (:15474-15493). Names arriving from the server are escaped. Save codes come from crypto.getRandomValues and are separate from the public UID. On the weak side, roguelite scores are self-reported by the client as {cleared, kills, dur} (rgSend, :21013) with only a plausibility check on time, and more than ten constant tables have to be kept in step with the server by hand.

codeCode Quality 4.5/10

Two opposite things are true. The comments are the best 'why' documentation in this audit: nearly every fix carries a date, the number that motivated it and what remains unsolved — a roundRect polyfill notes 4,565 error reports between 11-10 and 11-12 (:2915). And the structure is the worst: 18,841 lines of JavaScript in one global scope, 881 top-level functions, 920 lines longer than 300 characters and one of 9,475. The file documents the cost itself: one statement sat 'stuck to the tail of the previous line's comment and had never run', so the AI's inputs became NaN and it froze (:5146). At 2.66 MB the file is also the entire first load.

experimentTesting 3/10

Nothing testable ships. Comments refer to private tools — tools/rb_netcheck.cjs, which reproduced a desync ('88 desyncs and 45 full resends in one match; zero after the fix', :7763), tools/guard_check.mjs and tools/ai/sim.js — but they cannot be read or re-run from outside. What substitutes for tests in production is telemetry: up to three distinct error messages per visit are reported with the heartbeat, and an A/B framework exists, currently running only an A/A check. That is monitoring rather than verification, and it is scored as such.

verifiedCompleteness 9/10

By feature count the most complete product in this audit: instant play, a tutorial, local two-player, online rooms with invite links and QR codes, spectating, ranked with ban/pick, gym ladders, a roguelite mode with a daily challenge, four-player co-op and a practice room; plus a shop, daily quests, achievements, cloud saves, a PWA, an Android wrapper and nine static pages including a privacy policy and a changelog. The operational detail stands out: sw.js exists largely to deal with tabs left open on an old build, reloading them through the service worker only when no match is in progress, and its comment records roughly 1,956 devices online in an hour of which at least 170 were stale (sw.js:10-31). Four environments are separated by hostname and the debug switches are inert on production.

emoji_objectsOriginality 8/10

Not a re-skin of an existing game. The subject is a 2026 internet meme — a folk-dance teacher calling out move names — and the mechanics follow from it: the three shouted moves are the three starter attacks, and the animation is keyed from the source footage. The surrounding systems (roguelite ladder, gacha cosmetics, Elo) are genre-standard, but assembling them around a rollback fighter with trained bots, in four weeks, is not. The deduction is for what is borrowed without a stated basis: the voice lines are the original video's audio (:2951), and several fighters are modelled on recognizable public figures.

trending_upHighlights

  • Rollback netcode in a browser — deterministic 60 Hz simulation, key-bitmask exchange, seeded PRNG swap, float quantization, FNV state hashes and a full-resend fallback (index.html:14097-14235)
  • 'A lag spike only hurts the one lagging' — a late input is committed as 'not pressed' only when this side's own ping to the server shows its network is healthy (:14101, rbCommitTo :14231)
  • Self-play-trained opponents with a reaction delay — a hand-written MLP forward pass that sees the board 0.2 s late, weakened at low ranks by limiting attack frequency rather than by random input (:7906, :7982, :19347)
  • Skeletal animation with no art assets — a 16-angle pose vector and Catmull-Rom interpolation, keyed from the source video (:3293, :3405)
  • A service worker used to retire stale tabs — the only script the browser updates on its own is used to reload old pages, but never mid-match (sw.js:10-66)

trending_downWeaknesses

  • Ranked bots presented as players — when no human is matched within ten seconds a CPU is substituted and, per the code comment, 'dressed as a real player (name, look and scoring identical)' (:19341), with a name generator tuned so it does not give itself away (:19373); the FAQ, how-to-play and privacy pages checked do not mention it
  • One 2.66 MB file, one global scope — 881 top-level functions and a longest line of 9,475 characters; the layout has already produced a real bug where code glued to a comment never executed (:5146)
  • Source not public, no licence — the simulation, training and netcode check tools the comments cite all live in a private tree, so none of the quoted numbers can be reproduced
  • Unstated basis for audio and likenesses — voice lines are the source video's audio (:2951) and several fighters are modelled on recognizable public figures, on a site that also carries ads; no licence or permission statement was found. This is a noted risk, not a finding of infringement
  • Client and server constants synced by hand — a comment admits the server 'does not have these stages yet (the save guard will strip them from saves)' (:10859)

No repo, but the source is the page

No repository for this game could be found, and a third-party mirror on GitHub explicitly excludes the server. Yet this is one of the most readable projects in the audit, because the shipped index.html is not minified: 21,725 lines with every comment intact, in Chinese, dated to the day.

Those comments work as an incident log. They record what broke, how often, what was tried and what is still unsolved: a polyfill with its error count, a netcode fix with its before-and-after desync numbers, a service-worker rule with the number of stale devices it was written for. They also record the author's own security findings against their own code — the kind of note most projects keep private, if they write it at all.

The limit of this kind of reading is that comments are testimony, not evidence. The simulation runs, the training pipeline and the server-side save guard are all described and none can be inspected. Scores here reward what the shipped file demonstrates and discount what it only reports.

smart_toyAI-assisted development

AI review is named in the code: three comments dated 11-11 are tagged '(Opus 審查)' — Opus review — next to the fix each one prompted (:14722, :15066, :15493), and other comments refer to a security audit run, a master review list and a numbered bug table. Comments are also signed with several short handles, including 'owner' and 'junior'; whether those are people or agent roles cannot be determined from the artifact. With no commit history, the share of AI-written code is unknown. The opponents are a separate matter: they are small neural networks trained by self-play, which is machine learning inside the game rather than AI assistance in building it.

Verdict

The most technically ambitious project in this audit — rollback netcode, trained opponents and procedural animation, shipped in about four weeks as an operating product with ranked play and an app wrapper, and annotated with rare candour. It is held down by a single-file structure that has already cost it bugs, by tools and server code no outsider can check, and by a ranked ladder that fills empty seats with bots it does not tell players about.
